Importation of food and beverage products into the Philippines is highly regulated to protect public health and ensure product safety. Businesses must comply with requirements from multiple government agencies, including the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the Bureau of Customs (BOC), and other regulatory bodies depending on the type of product. These regulations are designed to ensure that all imported food items meet local safety standards before they are distributed in the market.



To legally operate, food importers are typically required to secure a License to Operate (LTO) from the FDA and obtain a Certificate of Product Registration (CPR) for each product they intend to import. Compliance with labeling requirements is also critical, as products must clearly indicate ingredients, nutritional information, and manufacturer details in accordance with Philippine standards. In many cases, businesses must also secure import permits from the Philippines for regulated goods, particularly those subject to sanitary and phytosanitary controls, before shipment is initiated.



From a customs standpoint, importers must follow the formal customs clearance process, which includes lodging the Goods Declaration, ensuring accurate tariff classification, and paying the appropriate duties and taxes. Proper import documentation is essential at every stage, as incomplete or incorrect paperwork can lead to shipment delays or additional inspections. Documents such as invoices, packing lists, certificates of origin, and permits must be complete and consistent to avoid issues during customs processing.



Common compliance challenges include improper labeling, missing product registration, and failure to secure necessary permits prior to importation. These issues can result in delays, penalties, or even rejection of goods at the border. Because food importation involves both regulatory compliance and customs procedures, businesses must adopt a well-coordinated approach to manage risks effectively. Companies that are new to the process often benefit from expert guidance to ensure full compliance and a smoother, more efficient importation experience.



## [Import Permits Philippines](/articles/import-permits-government-agencies-philippines)



**AGENCIES INVOLVED**



Food imports are regulated by multiple agencies depending on the product:



- FDA (Food and Drug Administration): processed foods, beverages, supplements
- DA agencies:
- BPI (plants, fruits, vegetables)
- BAI / NMIS (meat, animal products)
- BFAR (fish, seafood)
- Bureau of Customs (BOC): clearance & duties



**WHAT TO DO**



**Step 1: Register the Business**



**They must be a legal importer:**



- DTI/SEC registration
- BIR registration
- Importer accreditation (BOC)



**Step 2: Get FDA License to Operate**



**If importing processed food or beverages, they need:**



- FDA LTO (License to Operate) as Importer/Distributor



**Step 3: Product Registration**



For most food products:



- Certificate of Product Registration (CPR) from FDA
- Ensures product safety, labeling compliance, etc.



**Step 4: Secure Import Permit**



Depending on product type:



- FDA clearance / permit
- SPS Import Clearance (SPSIC) for agricultural goods
- Issued by DA agencies (BPI, BAI, BFAR)



Note: Import permits must be obtained before shipment leaves origin country.



**Step 5: Export Country Documents**



They need:



- Health certificate / sanitary certificate
- Phytosanitary certificate (for plant products)



**Step 6: Customs Clearance**



At arrival:



- Submit all permits & documents
- Pay duties & taxes
- Inspection by authorities



Note: All imported goods are subject to duties unless exempt.



**KEY LIST OF REQUIREMENTS**



Typical requirements:



- FDA License to Operate (LTO)
- Certificate of Product Registration (CPR)
- Import Permit / SPSIC
- Commercial Invoice
- Packing List
- Bill of Lading / Airway Bill
- Certificate of Origin
- Health/Phytosanitary Certificate



## [Customs Clearance Process](/articles/customs-clearance-process-in-the-philippines-explained)



Once a shipment arrives in the Philippines, it does not go directly to the warehouse. Instead, it undergoes customs clearance handled by the Bureau of Customs to ensure proper declaration and compliance with regulations.



Here’s how that process typically works.



**1. Before the arrival.**



**Clearance starts before the shipment even lands:**



They should already have:



- FDA License to Operate (LTO)
- Certificate of Product Registration (CPR) (for processed foods)
- Import Permit / SPSIC (for regulated goods)
- Shipping documents prepared



**2. Arrival of Shipment**



When goods arrive (port or airport):



- Carrier submits manifest to Bureau of Customs (BOC)
- Shipment is logged into the system (e.g., e2m / customs system)



**3. Filing the Goods Declaration**



The broker files the Goods Declaration electronically. This is where all shipment details are submitted to customs.



Documents submitted:



- Commercial Invoice
- Packing List
- Bill of Lading / Airway Bill
- Import Permit (FDA / DA)
- CPR (if required)
- Certificate of Origin
- Sanitary/Phytosanitary Certificate



If there are errors here, they tend to surface later so this step needs to be done carefully.



**4. Selectivity Lane**



Customs uses a system that assigns shipments to different “lanes”:



- Green Lane - released with minimal checks
- Yellow Lane - documents are reviewed
- Orange Lane - documents are reviewed, subject to xray scan.



If the image is found to be suspicious it will be subject to physical inspection.



- Red Lane - documents are reviewed, xray scan, and shipment is physically inspected



**5. Inspection and Evaluation**



Depending on product type:



**a. CUSTOMS EXAMINATION**



- Check quantity, value, classification



**b. FDA / DA INSPECTION**



- Labeling compliance
- Expiry dates
- Packaging condition



**c. POSSIBLE SAMPLING**



- Laboratory testing (for safety/contamination)



**d. PAYMENT OF DUTIES AND TAXES**



- Import Duty (varies by product)
- VAT (usually 12%)
- Other charges (if applicable)



**e. Release of Goods**



After Clearance:



- BOC issues Release Order
- Goods are released from port/warehouse
- Transported to your warehouse



**f. Post- Clearance Monitoring**



Authorities may still:



- Conduct inspections
- Audit documents
- Check market compliance



## [Import Documentation Checklist](/articles/import-documentation-checklist)



One of the most common reasons shipments get delayed at customs is incomplete or inconsistent documentation.



Before your shipment arrives, it’s important to make sure everything is complete and aligned. Customs will rely heavily on these documents when reviewing your declaration.



Here are the key documents that are typically needed.



**Core Documents**



Commercial Invoice



This shows:



- Seller and buyer details
- Description of goods
- Unit price and total value



Note: This is the main basis for customs valuation.



**Packing List**



Provides:



- Breakdown of packages
- Quantity per item
- Weight and dimensions



Note: This helps customs verify the physical shipment.



**Bill of Lading / Airway Bill**



Serves as:



- Proof of shipment
- Transport document
- Reference for cargo release



**Supporting Documents**



Depending on the shipment, they may also need import permits required for regulated goods and issued by agencies such as:



- Food and Drug Administration
- Department of Agriculture
- Department of Trade and Industry



Note: Agencies above are just examples among many others.



**Certificate of Origin**



Used to:



- Determine tariff rates
- Apply preferential trade agreements



**Insurance Documents**



If applicable, used for valuation purposes. This is also used in computing Duties and Taxes.



**Practical Tips**



- Make sure product descriptions are clear, specific and be generous on the details.
- Ensure values are consistent across documents.
- Avoid generic terms like “parts” or “equipment”.
- Double-check quantities and weights.



These documents are only among many other required documents for importing goods. This can be a hassle to keep track of for business owners.



Good documentation doesn’t just help with compliance, it speeds up clearance and reduces the risk of issues later on. One missing document may lead to penalties, or delays in customs clearance.
