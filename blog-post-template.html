Before You Ship: A Founder's Checklist for First-Time Philippine Imports

Most first-time importers lose time and money before the shipment ever reaches a Philippine port. The cause usually isn't slow customs, but because the groundwork wasn't done before the purchase order was final. The requirements that actually decide how smoothly a shipment moves are usually set weeks earlier: who the importer of record is, what permits the product needs, and whether the paperwork matches what customs will see on arrival.

None of this is complicated on its own. It just has to happen in the right order, and it rarely gets a founder's full attention until something has already gone wrong. Here's the sequence that tends to keep a first shipment out of trouble.

1. Confirm who the importer of record actually is

Before anything else, the entity bringing the goods in needs to be settled, such as a Philippine subsidiary, a distributor, or another qualified arrangement. This decision shapes everything downstream: which BOC accreditation applies, whose name goes on the customs entry, and who carries the tax exposure.

The business structure should be aligned with the import transaction before the first purchase order is placed, not adjusted around it afterward.

2. Map the product against its actual regulator

The commercial name of a product rarely tells you who regulates it. Classification depends on composition, intended use and tariff treatment, not the marketing copy. A supplement, for instance, might fall under FDA review, agricultural clearance, or both, and the answer changes what has to happen before the goods can leave origin.

Questions worth answering early

  • Does the product require an establishment license, a product-specific registration, or both?
  • Are there sanitary, phytosanitary or safety-certification requirements tied to the category?
  • Is the tariff classification settled, or does it need a formal review?

3. Get the documents consistent before they're printed

Customs holds are disproportionately caused by documents that don't agree with each other: an invoice description that doesn't match the permit, a value that doesn't reconcile, or a label that says something the registration doesn't cover. Reconciling this before the goods ship is far cheaper than correcting it after arrival.

4. Plan the delivery, not just the clearance

Getting cargo released from customs custody is not the same as getting it to where it needs to be. Inland transport, storage conditions, and any special handling should be arranged before the vessel or aircraft departs, not figured out while the cargo is already sitting at the port accruing charges.

None of this requires guessing. It requires sequencing, and a pre-shipment review that treats the first import as what it is: the transaction every future shipment gets measured against.
