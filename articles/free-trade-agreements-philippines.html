Free Trade Agreements in the Philippines

Apr 25, 2026 · 6 min read

Free Trade Agreements (FTAs) in the Philippines play an important role in facilitating international trade by reducing or eliminating tariffs on qualifying imported goods. The country participates in various regional and bilateral agreements designed to encourage smoother trade flows and improve economic cooperation with partner nations. For importers, these agreements can significantly lower costs and increase competitiveness in the local market, making them a valuable tool in global sourcing and supply chain planning.

To benefit from preferential tariff treatment under FTAs, importers must comply with specific requirements, particularly the rules of origin. These rules determine whether a product is considered to have originated from a member country and therefore qualifies for reduced duties. Proper documentation, such as a certificate of origin, must be submitted to customs authorities to support claims for preferential rates. Without these requirements, shipments may lose eligibility and be charged standard import duties instead.

A strong understanding of tariff classification is essential when using FTAs, as goods must be correctly classified to determine applicable tariff rates and eligibility under specific agreements. Incorrect classification can result in delays, reassessment of duties, or denial of benefits. Businesses should also apply effective duty optimization strategies, which involve legally minimizing import costs by maximizing FTA benefits, ensuring correct valuation, and selecting the most favorable sourcing arrangements.

Integrating FTAs into a broader import strategy allows businesses to make more informed sourcing and logistics decisions. Companies that align procurement with FTA-partner countries can reduce long-term costs and improve supply chain efficiency. However, failure to comply with documentation requirements or mismanagement of origin declarations can lead to penalties and loss of preferential treatment. By combining compliance awareness with strategic planning, importers can fully leverage FTAs in the Philippines to enhance operational efficiency and competitiveness.

Duty Optimization

What is duty optimization?

Duty optimization is about legally minimizing the total taxes and duties the importer pays when importing goods.

HOW TO OPTIMIZE DUTIES?

1. LEGAL FRAMEWORK

Duty optimization must comply with:

  • Customs Modernization and Tariff Act (R.A 10863)
  • WTO Trade Agreements
  • Bureau of Customs Regulations

2. CUSTOMS VALUATION METHODS

When to Use Each Method

Method 1: Transaction Value

Use when there is a sale for export, no major restrictions on use, no unrelated conditions or proceeds to the seller, and the price is not materially influenced by a buyer - seller relationship.

Method 2: Transaction Value of Identical Goods

Use if Method 1 cannot be applied, using the price of identical goods exported to the same country at about the same time.

Method 3: Transaction Value of Similar Goods

Use if Method 2 is not applicable, using the price of similar goods (similar in characteristics, materials, and function).

Method 4: Deductive Value Method

Use when Methods 1-3 fail, by starting from the price at which the imported goods (or identical/similar goods) are sold in the importing country, then deducting costs like commissions, profits, and some further expenses.

Method 5: Computed Value Method

Use when Methods 1-4 fail, by calculating value from the cost of materials and fabrication, plus profit and general expenses in the producing country.

Method 6: Fallback Method

Use only as a last resort when all other methods fail, applying a flexible version of Methods 1-5, but always in line with generally accepted accounting principles.

When to Move to the Next Method

  • Move away from Method 1 if there is no sale for export or if its conditions are not met.
  • Apply Methods 2 through 6 in strict order, except that Methods 4 and 5 may be swapped at the importer’s discretion.

3. USE OF TRADE AGREEMENTS

The Philippines is part of:

  • Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP): Entered into force in 2022, this includes ASEAN, Australia, China, Japan, New Zealand, and South Korea, covering goods, services, and investments.
  • ASEAN Free Trade Agreements: As an ASEAN member, the Philippines has agreements with:
  • China (ACFTA): Comprehensive Economic Cooperation.
  • Japan (AJCEP): Comprehensive Economic Partnership.
  • South Korea (AKFTA): Free Trade Agreement.
  • India (AIFTA): Free Trade Agreement.
  • Australia-New Zealand (AANZFTA): Free Trade Agreement.
  • Philippines-EFTA Free Trade Agreement: An agreement with Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway, and Switzerland that eliminates duties on industrial products.
  • Philippines-Japan Economic Partnership Agreement (PJEPA): A bilateral agreement.
  • Philippines-Korea Free Trade Agreement: Recently signed bilateral deal.

A key component of duty optimization is origin planning. This involves sourcing goods or raw materials from FTA member countries and ensuring that production processes meet the required rules of origin (ROO) thresholds, such as:

  • Regional Value Content (RVC)
  • Change in Tariff Classification (CTC)
  • Specific processing requirements

In addition, the importer should:

  • Conduct pre-importation audits to confirm eligibility
  • Coordinate with suppliers to secure valid Certificates of Origin (CO Form D, AJ, RCEP, etc.)
  • Maintain complete documentation for post-entry verification by the Bureau of Customs

Tariff Classification

Tariff classification is fundamental to determining whether an imported product qualifies for reduced duties under Philippine FTAs. Every product must be assigned the correct Harmonized System (HS) Code, which directly affects:

  • The base Most-Favored-Nation (MFN) duty rate
  • The applicable preferential tariff rate under an FTA
  • The specific rules of origin requirement

Misclassification is one of the most common reasons for denial of FTA benefits. Even if a product qualifies based on origin, an incorrect HS code can result in:

  • Higher duty assessments
  • Penalties and surcharges
  • Delays in customs clearance
  • Post-clearance audits and reassessments

To ensure accuracy, the importer should:

  • Conduct a technical product analysis (materials, composition, function, and use)
  • Refer to the Philippine Tariff and Customs Code (TCCP) and ASEAN Harmonized Tariff Nomenclature (AHTN)
  • Request advance rulings from the Bureau of Customs when classification is uncertain
  • Align HS classification with supplier documentation and certificates of origin

Proper tariff classification is also essential for verifying compliance with rules of origin, as many ROO criteria are based on changes at specific HS code levels (e.g., 4-digit or 6-digit). Accurate classification ensures that you can confidently claim preferential rates and avoid costly compliance issues.

Import Strategy

An effective import strategy leverages free trade agreements as a core cost-reduction tool rather than an afterthought. Businesses importing into the Philippines should design their procurement and logistics processes specifically to qualify for preferential tariff treatment.

A strong import strategy includes:

  • Supplier qualification: Verifying that suppliers can meet origin requirements and provide valid certificates of origin
  • Documentation planning: Ensuring all required paperwork is complete, accurate, and submitted on time
  • Cost-benefit analysis: Comparing MFN vs. FTA rates to confirm actual savings
  • Trade agreement selection: Choosing the most beneficial FTA when multiple agreements apply to the same product

They should also monitor:

  • Updates in tariff schedules and FTA provisions
  • Changes in customs regulations and enforcement practices
  • Expiry and validity of certificates of origin

Additionally, they can enhance your import strategy through:

  • Supply chain optimization: Adjusting manufacturing or assembly locations to meet origin thresholds
  • Risk management: Preparing for audits and verification requests from customs authorities
  • Digital trade management tools: Automating classification, documentation, and eligibility checks
